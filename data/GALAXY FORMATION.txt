GALAXY FORMATION
Introduction
Galaxy formation is the process through which enormous collections of stars, gas, dust, dark matter, and planets develop over billions of years. Galaxies are among the largest structures in the universe and serve as the homes of stars, planetary systems, nebulae, and black holes. Understanding how galaxies form helps astronomers explain the evolution of the universe from the Big Bang to the present day.

The Milky Way, our home galaxy, contains hundreds of billions of stars and is just one of an estimated two trillion galaxies in the observable universe. Modern astronomy seeks to understand how these vast structures originated, evolved, and continue to change over cosmic time.

History of Galaxy Formation Research
The study of galaxy formation developed gradually.

Early Observations
Ancient astronomers observed the Milky Way as a faint band of light across the sky.

1920s
Astronomer Edwin Hubble demonstrated that spiral nebulae were actually separate galaxies located far beyond the Milky Way.

Mid-20th Century
Astronomers began developing theories about galaxy formation based on gravity and cosmic evolution.

Modern Era
Space telescopes, computer simulations, and advanced observatories have provided detailed insights into galaxy formation processes.

Today, galaxy formation is one of the most important fields in cosmology and astrophysics.

The Universe After the Big Bang
Galaxy formation began shortly after the Big Bang.

Approximately:

13.8 billion years ago

The early universe was:

Extremely hot

Extremely dense

Filled with particles and radiation

As the universe expanded:

Temperatures decreased

Matter began forming

Atoms were created

Eventually, gravity started shaping matter into larger structures.

Role of Dark Matter
Dark matter plays a critical role in galaxy formation.

Characteristics:

Invisible

Does not emit light

Interacts mainly through gravity

Scientists estimate that:

About 85% of all matter in the universe is dark matter

Dark matter formed massive halos that acted as gravitational foundations for future galaxies.

Without dark matter, galaxies might never have formed.

Formation of the First Structures
Tiny density fluctuations existed in the early universe.

Some regions contained slightly more matter than others.

Gravity amplified these differences.

Over time:

Matter accumulated

Clouds became larger

Dark matter halos formed

These halos became the seeds of future galaxies.

Formation of the First Stars
Inside dark matter halos:

Gas accumulated

Hydrogen clouds collapsed

The first stars formed approximately:

100–200 million years after the Big Bang.

Characteristics:

Extremely massive

Very hot

Short lifetimes

These stars are known as Population III stars.

They produced the first heavy elements in the universe.

Birth of Protogalaxies
As stars formed, groups of stars and gas clouds assembled into protogalaxies.

Characteristics:

Small

Irregular shapes

Active star formation

Protogalaxies merged repeatedly over time.

These mergers created larger and more complex galaxies.

Hierarchical Galaxy Formation
Modern theory suggests galaxies grow through hierarchical formation.

Process:

Small structures form first.

Small galaxies merge.

Larger galaxies develop.

Massive galaxy clusters emerge.

This process continues today.

The Milky Way itself has absorbed many smaller galaxies throughout its history.

Galaxy Mergers
Galaxy mergers are fundamental to galaxy evolution.

When galaxies collide:

Stars rarely collide directly

Gas clouds interact

New stars form

Structures change dramatically

Results may include:

Larger galaxies

Starburst activity

Black hole growth

Major mergers can transform spiral galaxies into elliptical galaxies.

Star Formation in Galaxies
Galaxies continuously produce new stars.

Star formation depends on:

Gas availability

Density

Temperature

Galactic environment

Spiral arms are often active star-forming regions.

Young stars contribute to galaxy evolution through radiation and stellar winds.

Supermassive Black Holes
Most galaxies contain supermassive black holes at their centers.

Characteristics:

Millions to billions of solar masses

Examples:

Sagittarius A* in the Milky Way

These black holes influence galaxy evolution through:

Gravity

Radiation

Energy outflows

Scientists believe galaxies and black holes evolve together.

Active Galactic Nuclei
Some galaxies contain highly active central regions.

Known as:

Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN)

Characteristics:

Extremely bright

Powered by black holes

Emit enormous energy

Quasars are among the most powerful AGN.

These objects provide information about galaxy growth.

They form larger structures:

Groups
Collections of dozens of galaxies.

Clusters
Hundreds or thousands of galaxies.

Superclusters

Summary
Galaxy formation is the process by which vast collections of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter develop and evolve over billions of years. Beginning with tiny density fluctuations after the Big Bang, galaxies grew through gravitational collapse, star formation, and repeated mergers. Today, galaxies remain dynamic systems that continue to evolve, providing astronomers with valuable insights into the history and future of the universe. Understanding galaxy formation is essential for explaining how cosmic structures emerged and how the universe became the complex place we observe today.
