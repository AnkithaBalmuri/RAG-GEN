DARK MATTER

Introduction

Dark Matter is a mysterious form of matter that does not emit, absorb, or reflect light. Although it cannot be observed directly, its gravitational effects reveal that it makes up approximately 27 percent of the universe.

History of Discovery

In the 1930s, astronomer Fritz Zwicky observed that galaxies in clusters moved too quickly to be held together by visible matter alone. Later observations by Vera Rubin showed that stars in galaxies rotated faster than expected. These findings provided evidence for dark matter.

Evidence for Dark Matter

Galaxy Rotation Curves

Stars far from galactic centers move faster than predicted by visible matter alone.

Gravitational Lensing

Dark matter bends light through gravity, allowing astronomers to map its distribution.

Galaxy Clusters

The motions of galaxies within clusters require far more mass than visible matter provides.

Cosmic Microwave Background

Measurements of the early universe support the existence of dark matter.

Possible Candidates

Scientists have proposed:

* WIMPs (Weakly Interacting Massive Particles)
* Axions
* Sterile neutrinos
* Primordial black holes

Role in the Universe

Dark matter acts as a gravitational framework around which galaxies form and evolve.

Current Research

Experiments such as Xenon, LUX-ZEPLIN, and observations from space telescopes continue searching for dark matter particles.

Interesting Facts

* Dark matter cannot be seen directly.
* It outweighs ordinary matter by roughly five to one.
* Its true nature remains unknown.

Summary

Dark matter is one of the greatest mysteries in modern physics and astronomy. Understanding it may revolutionize our knowledge of the universe.
